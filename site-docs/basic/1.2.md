# § 1.2. Time and Delay

Every part in CircuitSim changes its outputs some time after its inputs
change. This page explains where those delays come from, what one unit of
simulated time means, and how the rest of the site states timing. Component
pages do not repeat it. Where a part's timing has something of its own to
show (a carry chain, a setup time, an access chain), its page ends the
implementation with a **Timing** paragraph and the few paths that matter.

## Simulated time

The simulator keeps one global time, counted in **ticks**. A change on a
wire is an event at a tick; a part that sees an input change computes its
new outputs and schedules them for a later tick. Nothing moves between
events, and there is one clock domain: every clock is a signal in the same
time line.

## Timing profiles

A **timing profile** decides how long each leaf part takes and what a tick
is. A leaf part is one with no gate-level form of its own: the logic gates,
the bus splitter, and the memory primitives that stand for electrical
behavior (bitlines, sense amplifiers, charge storage). Every larger part takes
its timing from its gates.

| Profile | A tick is | Gate delays | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `unit` | An abstract step | Every gate one tick | Default; all example tables on this site |
| `embedded` | 1 ps | 65 nm logic, an inverter driving four copies of itself (FO4) in 30 ps | Microcontroller-class chips |
| `desktop` | 1 ps | 32 nm logic, FO4 21 ps | Desktop-class chips |

The realistic profiles scale each gate by its fanout-of-four delay in FO4
units, from the logical-effort method: NAND 1.35 FO4, NOR 1.55 FO4, XOR 4
FO4, and AND or OR as a NAND or NOR followed by an inverter. Wires and the
splitter cost nothing in every profile. (`unit` charged the splitter one tick
until 2026-10-27; it is wiring, and with that tick a regrouping of the same
gates into word gates timed differently.)

One profile is active for a whole run. Programs and tools read it from the
environment variable `CIRCUITSIM_TIMING_PROFILE` (default `unit`). The
[interactive simulator](../visualizer.md) has a **Timing** selector beside the
scenario, kept in the page address as `?timing=embedded`; it shows times in
ps, ns, µs or ms when the profile's tick has a length, and in ticks for
`unit`. The example tables on component pages are recorded in `unit`, so
their times count gate delays.

## Gates

A gate publishes its new output one gate delay after an input changes. The
delay is **inertial**: if the inputs change back before a pending output has
been published, the pending change is withdrawn. A pulse shorter than the
gate's delay therefore does not pass through it, as in a real gate.

A part built from gates has no declared delay of its own. Its outputs settle
when the last gate on the path that changed settles, so the time depends on
the data: an adder whose carry stops after two positions settles sooner than
one whose carry runs the full width.

## Compact parts

Fidelity and timing are properties of a concrete component configuration.
For a word component (a [word gate](1.3.md#137-andgatew), a
[mux](1.5.md#151-muxnw) or a [chapter 2 unit](../alu/2.1.md)), width,
operation contract and adder topology identify that configuration. A structural family without
a matching behavioral factory cannot select the compact form; a delay table
for one width or topology does not describe another.

Most parts exist in two forms with the same pins and the same values: the
**structural** form built from smaller parts down to gates, and a
**behavioral** (compact) form that computes the result directly and is much
faster to simulate. A compact part must not be faster than its gates, so it
carries the delays proven on its gate-level form.

**Proven delay table.** For every input → output path, the analysis of the
gate-level form gives the earliest and the latest time the output can
change. A part with a control input (an ALU's operation, for example) has
one set of paths per control value. A clocked part adds clock → output
times, a setup time for each input, and the shortest clock period it can
take internally. The tables are generated for every profile by
`part_delay_tables` and checked against the gates. The full table of the
8-bit adder, for example:

**Delay twin.** Most compact parts also carry a flat list of their own
gates. When their inputs change they walk that list once with the current
values and find when each output is really final: a gate with an input at its
deciding value (0 into AND, 1 into OR) does not wait for its other input. The
part then publishes at that time, never later than its table allows. Parts
whose gate-level form contains loops, self-timed memory cells, or too many
gates use the table alone.

**The delay rule.** A compact part never publishes before its gates do, and
it should not be much later. The `DelayRuleTest` comparison runs both forms
of every part through the same situations and requires the gates' time to
be at least 70 % of the compact time on average and 90 % in the worst
measured case for each output, or a recorded exception with its measured
share. Parts that time themselves (DRAM, NOR flash, the SRAM device and their
controllers) are compared at the clock instead: same period, same cycles.

The compact form computes the final value once, so it does not reproduce the
short pulses (glitches) gates can make on the way. The option
`CIRCUITSIM_STRICT_SETTLING=1` shows X on such an output from the earliest
time it can move until its gates are final.

## Memories

Memories contain electrical behavior that gates cannot express: a bitline
discharging, a sense amplifier deciding, a DRAM cell restoring its charge, a
flash cell being programmed. Each such interval is a **delay line** whose
length comes from the profile, and grows with the number of rows where the
physics does (longer bitlines are slower). External chips take their
intervals from datasheets in the realistic profiles: SDRAM (Micron
MT48LC16M16A2) in `embedded`, DDR4 (Micron MT40A1G8) in `desktop`, and NOR
flash (Infineon S29GL256S) in both. Large memories cannot be built from
gates (the limit is 4,096 cells), so their tables are grown from the proven
64- and 128-word forms and say that they are estimates. The memory chapter
describes each technology's access in its
[Memory Timing](../memory/timing.md) section, with measured latencies per
profile.

## Clocks

A clocked circuit works only if every flip-flop's input is stable for its
setup time before each rising edge and for its hold time after it. The
**safe-clock analysis** reads the whole circuit, every gate or the proven
table of every compact part, and certifies the shortest clock period for
which every setup, hold and pulse-width requirement is met. A circuit it
cannot bound (for example a part that changes outputs at times of its own,
with no clock around it) gets no certificate.

## What is not modelled

- Wire delay between gates (except the long global wires of large memory
  arrays) and fan-out loading beyond the FO4 assumption.
- Process, voltage and temperature variation; every gate of one kind has the
  same delay.
- Power, analog noise, and metastability: a synchronizer that samples a
  changing input keeps its previous value instead of resolving at random.
- More than one clock domain.
